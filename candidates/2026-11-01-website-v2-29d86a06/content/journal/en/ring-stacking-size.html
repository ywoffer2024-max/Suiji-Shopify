Try the rings together, in the order you plan to wear them and on the intended finger. Two bands that fit separately can feel tight as a stack because they occupy more space along the finger or because one moves closer to the knuckle. Going up a size may help in some combinations. It can also make the new ring spin or slip when worn alone. Before changing sizes, work out which fit problem you want to solve.

Look at where each ring sits

Lay the rings side by side in the intended order. Band width is the space a ring takes up along your finger. Thickness is how far the metal rises above your finger. Inside diameter describes the opening of the ring. These are different measurements. If a listing gives you only “2 mm,” ask what was measured and where.

Here is a worked example. You wear a 2 mm band and want to add a 4 mm ring. If the sides are straight and meet, the rings occupy roughly 6 mm along your finger. That number describes their footprint. It does not give you a ring size or a number to add to your current size. Rounded bands, interlocking rings or rings with a gap can take up a different amount of space. If the band widens toward its setting, ask for its width where it will meet the neighboring ring as well.

GIA treats wide bands, rounded interiors and sets of rings separately in its sizing guidance. If a retailer offers a ring sizer, ask whether its gauges resemble the profiles you plan to wear. A measurement taken with a narrow gauge is a starting point for fitting a wider set. GIA, accurate determination of finger and ring sizes

Rehearse a few ordinary movements

Start with the ring you already wear. Put it on and remove it as you normally would. Pay attention to how it passes your knuckle and how it feels once seated. Open and close your hand, pick up your phone and type a few lines. Repeat with the second ring added. If a ring becomes hard to remove or the pressure bothers you, stop the trial rather than leaving it on to see whether you get used to it.

Reverse the order if the shapes allow. A wide band that feels fine at the base of the finger can become uncomfortable when a thin ring pushes it closer to the knuckle. If reversing the rings frees up movement, you have a practical option to compare before buying a different size. Check whether the settings lift each other as your finger bends. For a fitted pair designed to nest in a particular order, keep that order and try the pair together.

If you are undecided, repeat the trial another day with the same rings and finger. Note the time and what you were doing, such as working at your desk or coming back from a walk. Changing the order, the finger and the time all at once makes it hard to tell what made the difference. A short try-on can reveal a poor fit. It cannot predict how a stack will feel over a full day.

Fit the ring for how you will wear it

If you are between sizes, start with how often the new ring will be on its own. If you want to wear it alone on weekdays, include that in the try-on. If it is strictly a stacking ring, get fitted with the entire stack. If you are considering having rings joined permanently, ask the jeweler to account for the finished profile. Independent bands can shift relative to one another. A joined set behaves differently.

Imagine wearing a slim band on its own all week and adding a wide band when going out. With the wide one nearer the knuckle, you feel pressure when picking up your phone. At the base of the finger, the pressure eases, but the edges of the two rings lift against each other. Before sizing up, try a different profile or move the wide ring to another finger. If a larger size feels comfortable in the stack but the wide ring spins by itself, you know the trade-off you would be accepting.

The reverse situation happens too. Two slim rings might feel the same together and apart. If the stack goes on and comes off normally, and you can move your hand comfortably, adding a ring by itself is no reason to change sizes. Width tells you what to test. The try-on informs the choice.

Tell the seller what you are stacking when you order online

Give the hand and finger, the model you already own and the size as written on its receipt or the maker’s chart. Include its width in millimeters if you have it. Take a side-on picture of the existing ring on a flat surface and say whether the new one will also be worn alone. Ask whether the seller’s chart uses inside diameter, inside circumference or a system such as US ring sizes. Compare values on the chart for the model you are considering. Cartier’s own guide says sizing can vary by model and uses a similar-width ring for comparison. Cartier ring sizing guide, in French

Your message can be three sentences: “I wear this band on my right ring finger. I want to add your ring on the knuckle side and occasionally wear it by itself. What gauge or measurement should I use to check the fit in both configurations?” Ask about a fitting before engraving or any alteration. If the seller has no dimensions for the neighboring ring, advice by message will be approximate.

Frequently asked questions

Should I go up half a size for two rings?

There is no fixed adjustment. Try the rings in order and check the new ring on its own if you will wear it that way. Specify the sizing system: half a US ring size and half a millimeter are different units.

Is a printable ring sizer enough?

It can give you an initial reference if printed to scale. It will not reproduce the width or inside profile of a rigid band. For a wide or custom stack, bring the existing rings to a fitting.

What if the rings touch but still show a gap?

Look at the profiles and identify the first point of contact. A visible gap can come from the settings or the bands themselves. For custom work, ask to see a side view of both pieces before approving it. SUIJI’s guide to reviewing a jewelry CAD rendering

Can I use a ring from my other hand as the reference?

Use it as a starting point. Confirm the fit on the intended finger and hand in the same order, especially if the rings pass over the knuckles differently or there is less space along the finger.
