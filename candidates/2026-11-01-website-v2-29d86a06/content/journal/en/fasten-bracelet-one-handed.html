Find the step where you get stuck

Lay the bracelet on a clean cloth in good light. Find the receiving ring and the moving part of the clasp. Watch both as you open and release the lever. That gives you a reference for the wrist test. If the mechanism feels different from usual, a component looks bent, or the clasp no longer closes fully, stop the trial before wearing it.

A clasp can work off the wrist and still slip when you put it on. Break the action down. Can you keep the ring in view? Can you open the lever with the hand you actually use? Once those steps work, can you bring the ends together without pulling the chain taut? Then check whether you can take the bracelet off with the same free hand. With a flexible chain and a lobster clasp, start by positioning the ring end where you can see it. Rest your forearm and keep your wrist in a natural position. Turn your forearm slightly or change the bracelet’s orientation if the ring disappears under your hand. Work over the table so the bracelet lands on the cloth if it slips. Open the lever, guide the designated ring into the clasp, release the lever and visually check that the ring is captured before lifting your arm.

Watch how the bracelet opens while worn

A clasp’s name tells you something about its shape. You still need to see where your fingertip goes, which direction the release moves and how to tell that the clasp is closed. For a box clasp, ask the seller to show you the release and any secondary catch on the bracelet while it is worn. Try it yourself in an orientation that works for you. If a catch hides another control or one step needs two parts held in place, ask the seller to demonstrate that exact sequence. Avoid prying with tools or forcing a part that does not move as expected.

Ask for a complete demonstration before buying

In person, fasten and remove the bracelet as you would at home. Say which hand operates the clasp and whether you will wear a watch on that wrist. Online, ask for a close view of both ends of the closure, the usable length at each intended position, and a video showing how the bracelet goes on and comes off the wrist. A video that jumps from an open clasp to a closed bracelet leaves out the action you need to judge.

If you want to alter a bracelet you own, take it to a jeweler and describe the step that fails. Ask whether a different orientation, an easier target ring or a different closure would work with that chain and length. Try the proposed result for appearance and on-wrist removal before agreeing to a change. If the piece is convertible, confirm its intended configurations and fit too. Frequently asked questions

Is a larger lobster clasp easier to close?

It may give you a better grip if the lever is easy to reach. Try it with its receiving ring and intended orientation. A larger clasp alone cannot supply the room your thumb needs.

Should I add an extender?

Try a designated adjustment position first, if there is one. Check how you reach the clasp while fastening and how the bracelet sits once closed. If there is no alternative position, ask the maker or a jeweler what is compatible.

Can I clip the clasp into any link on the chain?

Use the points intended by the maker. A link can be large enough for the clasp to enter without being a suitable fastening point.

What if I can put it on but cannot take it off?

Support your forearm, check whether you need to release a secondary catch, and follow the maker’s instructions. If it is still stuck, get help or have the clasp inspected without forcing it.
