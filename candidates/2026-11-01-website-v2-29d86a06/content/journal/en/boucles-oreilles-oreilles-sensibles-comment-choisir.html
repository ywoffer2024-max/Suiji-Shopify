The short answer

Do not choose on the word “hypoallergenic” alone. Check the metal in the post and back, the surface treatment, the evidence attached to the actual component, the weight per earring, pressure points, intended use and return terms. No material guarantees that every person will be reaction-free.

This guide is for jewellery worn in a healed lobe. It cannot diagnose allergy, irritation or infection. Spreading redness, increasing pain, marked swelling, pus, fever, feeling unwell or embedded jewellery require prompt professional assessment. Do not remove a new piercing on your own when infection is suspected.

Eight checks that reduce uncertainty

  1. What exactly is the post made from?
  2. What are the back, solder and other contact parts made from?
  3. Is the colour integral, anodised, PVD-coated or plated?
  4. Does the document shown match the material lot or finished part?
  5. Is weight stated per earring?
  6. Can the post or back compress the lobe?
  7. Is the piece intended for a healed or healing piercing?
  8. Are hygiene-related return exclusions visible before checkout?

“Hypoallergenic” is not a medical guarantee

Ask which contact components were covered by the stated nickel-release evidence. Regulatory compliance and individual tolerance are different questions.

Claim Useful information it may convey What it does not prove alone
Hypoallergenic an intention to reduce allergenic risk composition or universal tolerance
Nickel free a claim about nickel the release result or every component
REACH compliant claimed regulatory compliance report scope, lot and individual tolerance

Inspect every contact component

The decorative front may not be the part that causes pressure or prolonged contact. Ask separately about the post, back, hoop, solder and coating. A certificate for titanium bar stock does not automatically cover a separately sourced back or a finished plated earring.

Read SUIJI materials by component and care guidance, then check the exact listing instead of treating the word titanium as a guarantee for the whole piece.

If the exact part or material is unclear, you can ask SUIJI a specific product question before placing an order.

For titanium described as ASTM F136 or ISO 5832-3, look for a chain from standard to mill certificate, heat number, component and finished product. These standards describe implant material; they do not certify the whole earring or promise personal tolerance.

Separate material reaction from mechanical discomfort

A tight back, short post, rough edge, heavy pendant or repeated snag can cause pain without proving allergy. Compare weight per earring, the distance of the mass from the piercing, usable post length, back shape, movement and the way the piece interacts with hair, phones, helmets and scarves.

There is no universal comfort weight. A small solid earring may pinch; a large open design may be relatively light.

A healing piercing needs different decisions

A fresh piercing is a healing wound. Material, size, finish, sterility and anatomical fit should be assessed by a qualified piercer. The Association of Professional Piercers lists material standards and surface requirements for initial jewellery. If infection is suspected, seek medical advice rather than following a generic retail instruction to remove the piece.

Titanium, steel and silver: what the name does not tell you

A metal name is the start of the specification, not the end of the decision. “Titanium”, “surgical steel” and “sterling silver” each leave questions that a useful product page should answer.

For titanium body jewellery, ASTM F136 and ISO 5832-3 are commonly cited material specifications for wrought Ti-6Al-4V ELI used in surgical implant applications. They describe material requirements; they do not certify a complete earring, guarantee personal tolerance or prove that a coloured coating uses the same material. Ask which component uses the documented titanium and how its batch is linked to the finished product.

“Surgical steel” is a broad commercial expression. Some stainless steels contain nickel, so an exact grade and evidence relevant to the finished contact components matter more than the adjective. Sterling silver normally means 92.5% silver plus other alloying metals. The remaining composition, solder, post and back can still differ, and sterling silver is not on the Association of Professional Piercers’ list of suitable materials for an initial piercing.

Three situations require three levels of evidence

The longer the contact and the newer the piercing, the more exact the specification should be.

Occasional wear on a fully healed lobe

Start with the material of the post and back, weight per earring, surface condition and return terms. A complete component list and clear scale images remove much of the uncertainty. If you have reacted before, save the product description and model name instead of relying on the colour or appearance of the metal.

All-day wear

Add geometry to the material check. A short post, tiny back or heavy element far from the hole may become uncomfortable after several hours. Look for millimetre dimensions, weight for one earring and a side-view photograph. A claim that an earring is “lightweight” is less useful than an actual measurement.

A new or healing piercing

Do not select initial jewellery from a fashion checklist alone. Anatomy, expected swelling, gauge, post length, thread design, surface finish, sterility and professional insertion all matter. Material documentation is necessary, but it cannot replace correct fitting by a qualified piercer.

How to audit a certificate without being a metallurgist

Useful evidence connects a document, a batch, a component and the product being sold. A generic PDF proves very little if the seller cannot explain that chain.

Suppose a listing says “gold PVD ASTM F136 titanium”. That combines at least three claims: a base material, compliance with a material specification and a deposited coating. A mill certificate may describe the raw titanium batch. It does not automatically document the coating’s composition, adhesion or wear. A nickel-release test on the post may not cover a separately sourced butterfly back.

Check five items:

  1. Who issued the document: a material producer, laboratory or other organisation?
  2. Which specification or test method and edition are named?
  3. Is the sample, heat or batch identifiable?
  4. Which part was assessed: post, back, coating or assembled earring?
  5. How does that reference match the exact variant you are buying?

A limited answer can still be honest. “This report covers the post only” is more trustworthy than presenting one certificate as proof of every component and every possible reaction.

Mechanical discomfort can mimic a material problem

Pressure, leverage, roughness and snagging can hurt even when the material is well documented. Compare weight per piece, post length, back surface, sharp edges and the location of the heaviest element.

There is no universal gram threshold that is comfortable for every ear. A large open design can be light, while a small solid earring can be dense. A back pressed tightly against the lobe can create a local mark independent of allergy. If one ear hurts and the other does not, consider sleeping position, phone use, glasses, hair and the angle of each piercing.

On a healed lobe, change one factor at a time after the skin has returned to normal. Do not run a self-test on a new piercing, broken skin or an area with possible infection.

If an old pair suddenly starts causing a reaction

A new reaction does not automatically mean that your body changed overnight. A coating may have worn, the surface may be scratched, a back may have been replaced with another metal or local swelling may have increased pressure.

For an easily removable earring on a healed lobe, stop wearing it, avoid piling on products and observe the area. Record when symptoms began, photograph the same area in comparable light and save the advertised composition. Recurrent reactions to several products are a reason to discuss contact allergy and patch testing with a dermatologist.

Seek professional care promptly for spreading redness, increasing pain, marked swelling, pus, fever, feeling unwell or embedded jewellery. Do not use an online materials guide to decide how to remove or treat a problematic new piercing.

FAQ

What is the best metal for sensitive ears?

There is no best metal for everyone. Precisely documented, well-finished implant-grade titanium is commonly considered for body jewellery, but component identity, construction, anatomy and personal history still matter.

Does REACH compliance mean allergy-free?

No. REACH limits nickel release for specified articles. It does not guarantee individual tolerance or exclude every cause of irritation.

Why can lightweight earrings still hurt?

A short post, tight back, rough edge or snag can hurt independently of total weight.

Should I change a painful new piercing myself?

Not without qualified advice. Contact a piercer for fit and a healthcare professional for medical warning signs.
